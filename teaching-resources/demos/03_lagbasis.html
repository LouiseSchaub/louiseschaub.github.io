Why the Lagrange Polynomials Are a Basis

For pairwise distinct nodes x₀, …, xₙ every Lᵢ is 1 at its own node and 0 at all others. That single property makes L₀, …, Lₙ linearly independent, lets them represent every polynomial in 𝒫ₙ, and makes the coordinates of a polynomial its values at the nodes. What to try

Read the dots in the top plot. Each Lᵢ has a filled dot at height 1 at its own node and hollow dots at height 0 at all others. Written as a vector of node values, Lᵢ is the unit vector eᵢ. This does not change when you drag the nodes.

Independence. Move the sliders: the combination Σ cᵢ Lᵢ passes through (xₖ, cₖ) at every node, because evaluating at xₖ switches off every term except cₖ Lₖ. The combination can only be the zero function if every dot sits at 0, i.e. all cᵢ = 0.

Spanning. Switch to "spanning" and choose q. With cᵢ = q(xᵢ) the residual r vanishes at all n + 1 nodes. If q has degree at most n, so does r, and a nonzero polynomial of degree ≤ n has at most n roots: r ≡ 0. Choose the cubic with n = 2: r still vanishes at the three nodes but is not zero, because q is not in 𝒫₂. Raise n to 3 and it works.

Let two nodes collide. Drag one node onto its neighbour. The denominators xᵢ − xⱼ become small, the two basis polynomials grow huge, and the Vandermonde matrix becomes nearly singular. At xᵢ = xⱼ both descriptions break down: the nodes must be pairwise distinct.
